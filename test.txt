Shading model; A computational model to approximate what colour to assign the pixel in the scene (i.e., “shade” all visible surfaces)<br> • Make use of an illumination model<br> • Input: point 𝑝 in the scene + scene (lights, objects, surface materials)<br> • Output: estimation of colour at 𝑝 due to its characteristics (properties like material, geometry), other objs around it and light source<br> • Two families of illumination model<br> • Local illumination: illuminate surface point through direct light source<br> • Global illumination: illuminate point with light transport from entire scene<br> Amt of light that hits pixel from light source determines what colour we shade the pixel;  
Lambert model; Lambertian model uses eqn colour = albedo * max(0, dot(N,L)) * light_intensity<br> Good for diffuse surfaces<br> Basically just models the intuition that surfaces facing light source are brighter, surfaces facing away/without direct view of light source are darker<br> albedo: value for how shiny surface is. Shinier surfaces reflect more light (so lighter), darker ones reflect less. So higher albedo val means whiter, lower means blacker<br> N: vector that’s the direction of the normal to the surface<br> L: vector that’s pointing at the light source. So it’s not dir of light, it’s the opposite<br> Taking the dot prod of these 2 gives cos theta which is angle between these 2 vecs<br> Val clamped to 0 bc u can’t have “neg light”<br> ^ All vals are RGB vecs<br> Then light intensity ofc is just intensity/strength of light source;  
Improvements upon naive raycasting & tracing: Spatial acceleration structures; Naive ray casting is very expensive!!!<br> N objects and r rays<br> O(N * R)<br> Need 2 call fewer intersection tests & make each call more efficient<br> -> Can do this w special data structs and algos<br> Fewer calls: hierarchical partitioning<br> More Efficient: bound objs w simpler primitives<br> “Spatial acceleration structures”: term to desc data struc and algos to do just this;  
2 important distinctions of Spatial acceleration structures; Object partitioning vs space partitioning<br> Regular (i.e., uniform) vs irregular (i.e., adaptive) space partitioning<br> Many diff types w pros and cons;  
Uniform grids; Simple space partitioning<br> No formula for “optimal grid size” u gotta test and check/finetune<br> Just reducing num of tests we need 2 perform, not actually making the algo itself more efficient<br> Split total 3d space into separate uniformly sized grids<br> Use snth like DDA to rasterize a line, then u only need to perform raycasting intersection tests on those grids (that the rasterized line confirmed to intersect with that particular path). Rasterization is way cheaper so u can use it as a pretest;  
Bounding volumes; Object bounding algo<br> Need to perform same num of tests, but we r making each one a little more efficient -> if u have 100 objs u still need to perform 100 tests<br> Bound complex objs (contains lots of meshes) w simple primitives<br> First test ray intersection on simple primitives that’s quicker to compute<br> Only if ray intersects simple primitive then do we test for intersection on complex obj that’s made up of primitives<br> When is this worthwhile?<br> • Simple shape as reasonable bounding volume (e.g., spheres, boxes)<br> • Contained object is complex - no reason to contain sphere w another sphere<br> • Good “tightness of fit” - Too much empty space will give many false positives, too good of a fit doesn’t effectively reduce complexity of og obj so there’s no point in having it anyways since that will b expensive to perform the pretesting on anyways<br> Multiple bounding volumes per obj is also possible but not focus of this method in vanilla form;  
Choice of bounding volume primitives; a) Spheres: pretty efficient easy to intersect, but usually not very tight fits<br> b) Axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABBs): reasonably tight fit and also still a primitive. However most objs prob not conveniently aligned w xyz axis/coord space. Most commonly used for most apps, reasonable trade off<br> c) Oriented bounding boxes (OBBs): axis aligned w the obj so it’s a tighter fit. Easy to intersect & tighter for arbitrary objects, but need to transform (orient the box) but ray intersection test is less efficient<br> Generally: tighter = intersection test more computationally expensive;  
AABBs Ray-slab intersection tests; Decompose ray intersection test for an AABB into 3 separate tests, each one along each axis for limits of bounding box<br> Extend top edge of box (y max) and y min infinitely, until they intersect the ray. Basically wanna find r(t).y = y1.<br> r(t) = o +td<br> Tymax = (y1 - o.y)/d.y<br> Once we have an interval in which the box lies for y, repeat for x. Take intersection of these 2 sets to see if there’s an area in space for which the box can exist – bc if the box intersects w ray, it must be within this intersection (doesn’t necessarily have to be true)<br> If no intersection of sets = no intersection of box w ray<br> Do for xyz;  
Bounding volume hierarchies (BVH); Hierarchy of smaller and smaller bounding boxes that partitions objs into many grps<br> Bounding volumes that contain grps of objs at a time<br> Each bounding volume can also contain other bounding volumes<br> Recursive repetition by starting off w 1 big box, then partitioning until each bounding volume contains smaller and smaller nums of objs until u reach desired min num, ex 2 objs per BV<br> Can test for intersection w each BV. Only if it intersects then do u have to check which child volume it is, otherwise u can disregard it entirely<br> Ex if the root node/main box doesn’t have intersections yay saves u a lot of time<br> Obj can only belong to 1 BV (not including BVs of BVs. like only 1 BV at the “same level”) at a time even if it intersects w/contained within multiple -> create grps first then BVs so which BV the obj belongs to depends on its group, not the physical BV<br> Has overlaps/inefficiencies bc we need obj to be entirely contained within its BV<br> Guaranteed to only have to test against the og objs at most once <- may need 2 perform tests on many many BVs but only once per og obj<br> Drawback: bc there may be overlap, 1st intersection may not be closest obj to camera<br> Expand tree if it intersects?? Go back 2 root and return all intersections;  
How to construct BVH partitions?; Ideally BVs should be as small as possible to reduce empty space (aka possibilities for false positives during ray intersection testing)<br> Finding optimal BVs is extremely hard problem, so we just rely on heuristics that give us reasonable partitions<br> Some heuristics:<br> Center partition: partition based on centre of coordinate system - simple and cheap but can create wildly unbalanced partitions<br> Median partition: need to sort all BVs by coords and partitions at median (ex: x median), more balanced but more expensive due to sorting<br> ^ these 2 can work well in simple scenes but in practice not often used. Instead we use more sophisticated: SAH;  
Surface Area Heuristic (SAH); Tries to approximate cost of performing ray intersection test<br> Kt (estimate of cost of performing ray intersection test) Ki (cost of actually intersecting w children BV) r constants so they don’t rly affect the heuristic. Calcs based on num of boxes, SA<br> Complicated formu spits out a num that tells u how good the partition is<br> Theorem: given box 𝐵 completely contained in box 𝐴, probability that ray traversing 𝐴 intersects 𝐵 is given by 𝐒𝐀(𝐵)/𝐒𝐀(𝐴)<br> Make a bunch of candidate partitions<br> • Compute expected ray traversal cost for a subtree of hierarchy<br> • Then, greedily choose partition that minimizes cost<br> Modern systems generally use more complicated algos but its the same idea: have a cost func and try to partition by minimising cost;  
• 𝑘-d trees (quadtree, octree); Adaptive space partition (not object partitioning like BVHs, not uniform subdivision like uniform grids);
